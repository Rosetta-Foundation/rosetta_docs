# Chapter Three
## Growing Up Between Eras

It is one thing to read about a historical transition. It is another to discover, years later, that you happened to grow up inside one. People living through periods of profound change rarely recognize them as such. History has the advantage of hindsight. Those experiencing it usually have only the ordinary routines of everyday life, unaware that future generations may eventually describe those same years as the beginning of something entirely new.

By the closing decades of the twentieth century, humanity's relationship with knowledge was quietly changing once again. Books remained indispensable, libraries continued to serve their communities, and universities still stood as remarkable centers of learning. Yet something new was beginning to emerge alongside these institutions. Personal computers were appearing in homes, schools, and offices. Telephone lines, once intended solely for conversation, were beginning to carry information as well as voices. Networks that had previously connected governments and universities were gradually becoming accessible to ordinary families. Looking back, it is tempting to see this period as the dawn of the Information Age. Living through it, however, it felt far less dramatic. The transformation arrived one household, one classroom, and one curious child at a time.

A few months ago, my mother and grandmother reminded me of something I had completely forgotten. My grandmother, who will celebrate her ninety-second birthday this November, recalled that whenever they visited my elementary school, there was one room I insisted on showing them before anything else. It was not the playground or my classroom. It was the computer lab. They remembered how excited I was to demonstrate the educational games we had been using, fascinated less by the games themselves than by the machines that made them possible. I have no memory of those visits, yet hearing their stories explained something about me that I had never fully appreciated. Long before I understood what software was, or why computers might matter, they had already captured my imagination.

By the time I reached my teenage years, that fascination had found a new outlet. My parents brought home our family's first computer with Internet access. It connected through AOL, a 56k dial-up modem, and a telephone line stretched through the house. On most days the connection settled somewhere around 16.6 kilobits per second, and while I was online no one else could use the phone. By modern standards the experience was painfully slow, often interrupted, and occasionally frustrating. At the time, however, none of that seemed to matter. Every successful connection felt as though a small doorway had opened into a world that had previously been inaccessible. It did not feel like using a machine. It felt like participating in something that was steadily becoming larger than any one computer, any one household, or any one person.

Not long afterward, I enrolled in a computer science class and wrote my first program in QBasic. Like many students, I expected programming to be another assignment, another subject to complete before moving on to something else. Instead, it quietly redirected the course of my life. Programming revealed that computers were not simply tools to operate but systems to understand, shape, and improve. Every program was an attempt to capture a piece of human reasoning and express it in a form that could be repeated, refined, and built upon. Looking back, I suspect that realization interested me even more than the computers themselves.

The decades that followed allowed me to watch software evolve from an exciting hobby into one of civilization's defining infrastructures. I learned more than fifteen programming languages and worked on desktop applications, websites, APIs, distributed systems, cloud platforms, enterprise software, and, eventually, artificial intelligence. During that time I witnessed technologies rise with extraordinary confidence, only to be replaced a few years later by ideas that seemed equally certain of their permanence. Frameworks succeeded one another. Architectural styles fell in and out of favor. Entire categories of software that once appeared indispensable gradually faded into history. Living through these transitions taught me something I had not expected. Technology changes far more quickly than the problems it attempts to solve.

For that reason, I have become cautious whenever someone declares that a new technology will change everything. History suggests that such predictions are rarely as straightforward as they first appear. Most innovations become important in ways their creators never anticipated, while others fade despite enormous enthusiasm. It is remarkably difficult to recognize a genuine turning point while standing within it.

Even so, looking back across those thirty years, one observation seems difficult to ignore. During my lifetime, access to information changed from something that was limited and often difficult to obtain into something that became increasingly ordinary. Search engines placed vast collections of knowledge within reach of anyone with an Internet connection. Encyclopedias gave way to websites that could be updated continuously. Open source communities shared software with people they would never meet. Universities published lectures online. Experts were no longer the only people capable of contributing to humanity's growing body of knowledge.

This transformation did not happen all at once, nor did it arrive because of any single invention. It emerged gradually through countless improvements made by engineers, educators, researchers, entrepreneurs, and communities around the world. Looking back, it is difficult not to feel a sense of gratitude for what they collectively accomplished. They helped create a world in which curiosity is no longer constrained as tightly by geography, wealth, or access to institutions as it once was. An extraordinary amount of human knowledge has become available to an extraordinary number of people.

That achievement, however, invites an interesting question. If preserving knowledge became easier, and accessing knowledge became easier, what challenge remained? As my career continued, I found myself returning to that question more often than I realized. At first, I could not quite explain why. I only knew that, despite having more information available than ever before, something still felt incomplete.
