# Chapter Four

## When Information Became Ordinary

[← Growing Up Between Eras](chapter-3.md) · [Contents](README.md) · [Next: The Fragility of Context →](chapter-5.md)

The incompleteness I could not yet name became clearer once information stopped feeling scarce. Early in my years as a software engineer, a significant portion of solving any technical problem still involved finding information in the first place. Documentation was often incomplete, books became outdated almost as soon as they were printed, and search engines were still learning how to navigate an expanding web. Useful answers might be buried within mailing lists, scattered across obscure web pages, or preserved only in the memories of colleagues who had encountered the same problem years before. Progress depended as much upon locating knowledge as it did upon understanding it.

That scarcity did not last. Search engines improved. Open source software flourished. Documentation became easier to publish and easier to update. Communities formed around nearly every programming language, framework, and technology imaginable. Questions that had once consumed an afternoon of searching could often be answered within minutes, and problems that had once required access to specialists became approachable by anyone willing to learn. The barriers separating curiosity from information had fallen farther than I suspect many of us appreciated while we were living through it.

What remained was a different kind of difficulty. It was no longer unusual to find dozens of articles explaining the same concept, each written from a different perspective, for a different audience, and with different assumptions about what the reader already understood. Documentation became more complete, but also more fragmented. Organizations accumulated years of valuable discussions across shared drives, wikis, repositories, ticketing systems, and chat applications, only to discover that much of what they knew remained surprisingly difficult to rediscover when it was needed most. The challenge was no longer locating information. It was understanding how all of that information fit together.

Information and understanding are closely related, but they are not the same thing. For most of the era I am describing, a search engine could retrieve documents quickly, but it could not easily explain how one idea builds upon another, why two respected experts disagree, which assumptions shaped a particular conclusion, or what historical context gives an observation its meaning. Facts rarely arrive as isolated pieces. They exist within larger networks of relationships, and it is often those relationships, rather than the individual facts themselves, that produce genuine understanding.

Perhaps this should not surprise us. Human beings have never learned primarily by collecting isolated facts. We learn by connecting them. A child gradually connects letters into words, words into sentences, and sentences into stories. Scientists connect observations into theories. Historians connect events into narratives. Engineers connect individual components into systems. Even our memories seem to work less like filing cabinets than webs of association, where one idea naturally calls another to mind. Understanding has always emerged from relationships at least as much as from information itself.

We have become good at making information available. We are still learning how to transform that abundance into understanding. That is the pressure left behind once access became ordinary — and it is a pressure I spent much of my career trying to work through, one connection at a time.
