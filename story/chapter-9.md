# Chapter Nine

## What a Practice of Paths Would Require

[← What Every Generation Leaves Behind](chapter-8.md) · [Contents](README.md) · [Next: A Name for the Work →](chapter-10.md)

If the obligation described in the previous chapter is real — if each generation owes the next not only conclusions, but enough of the journey that those conclusions can be understood — then a further question follows almost at once. What would it mean to take that obligation seriously as a practice rather than as a sentiment? Sentiments are easy to affirm and easy to forget. Practices endure because they shape ordinary habits: what people record when they decide, what they leave for strangers who will arrive later, what they deliberately allow to fade.

I do not mean a practice of preserving everything. That impulse is understandable, and in an age of cheap storage it can look almost responsible. Yet Chapter Seven already suggested why abundance of destinations can make paths harder to recover. A practice of paths would be selective in a different sense. It would ask, before a trail is marked, whether a future traveler would need this relationship in order to continue wisely — not whether the material might someday prove interesting. Much of what we say and write is provisional, personal, or already superseded. Forgetting remains merciful. The discipline lies in noticing which assumptions, alternatives, and sequences would become invisible the moment the people who held them moved on, and in choosing those for deliberate care.

Such a practice would also have to treat disagreement as part of the inheritance rather than as noise to be cleaned away. Many organizations preserve the decision that won and quietly discard the reasoning that lost. That habit produces tidy archives and fragile successors. A later mind that encounters only the surviving conclusion has no way to know which objections were already considered, which constraints were temporary, or which rejected options might become wise again when circumstances change. Traditions that keep layered commentary, dissenting opinions, or decision records with refused alternatives have already discovered this. A broader practice of paths would borrow their insight without pretending every workplace must become a Talmud or a court of law. It would simply refuse the fiction that a clean final answer is the same thing as an intelligible history.

There is a further requirement that is easy to overlook because it sounds almost social rather than technical. Paths are preserved for travelers who were not present when the trail was made. That means the practice cannot assume shared memory, shared vocabulary, or shared unspoken context. An engineer writing for colleagues who sat in the same meetings will omit precisely the relationships a newcomer most needs. A scientist writing for peers already steeped in a field's controversies will leave those controversies between the lines. A practice of paths would cultivate the habit of explaining enough that a careful stranger could enter the conversation — not everything, but enough. It is a form of hospitality across time.

I began to suspect that this hospitality is rarer than we admit because so many of our tools reward the opposite. We are excellent at capturing the state of things: the current document, the merged change, the published result. We are less consistent at capturing the sequence of considerations that made that state seem reasonable. Meeting notes often record what was said without recording which assumptions went unchallenged. Version histories show what changed without showing why an alternative was abandoned. Search can retrieve the surviving artifact without retrieving the web of influence that gave it meaning. None of this is a failure of diligence so much as a failure of attention. We have optimized for destinations because destinations are easier to name, store, and count.

A practice of paths would therefore ask people to leave behind a different kind of residue. Not more documents for their own sake, but clearer relationships among the documents that already matter: which question prompted which inquiry, which experiment closed which door, which conversation changed the meaning of a decision without rewriting its text. In families, this might look like explaining why a tradition exists rather than only insisting that it continue. In classrooms, it might look like showing the failed approaches that make a correct answer intelligible. In organizations, it might look like recording not only what was chosen, but which constraints, tradeoffs, and unresolved doubts still surround the choice. The forms would differ. The underlying discipline would not: leave enough of the journey that the next person need not begin as an archaeologist.

Perhaps the most important requirement is humility about authorship. A path preserved only as a monument to the person who walked it first is already halfway lost. Paths endure when later travelers can revise them, branch from them, and sometimes abandon them without losing the ability to understand why the earlier route once made sense. Continuity is not the same as permanence. A living inheritance remains open to correction. If a practice of paths ever hardened into a demand that every trail be frozen, it would have mistaken the museum for the road.

I do not claim that such a practice would be easy, or that any single profession has already perfected it. I claim only that once the obligation is taken seriously, the shape of the work becomes clearer. We would need habits of selective marking, of preserving disagreement alongside conclusion, of writing for travelers who were not in the room, and of leaving relationships among ideas in a condition others can travel. Tools may eventually help or hinder that work. They cannot define it. The practice comes first: a craft of making the path behind us a little more findable for people we will never meet.
